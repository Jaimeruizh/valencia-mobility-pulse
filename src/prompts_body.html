Build your first urban mobility dashboard with AI

Nine prompts that take you from four open data tables about València to a working dashboard. You write no code. You decide what the dashboard should answer, and you check that it tells the truth.

Before you start

  • Open an AI assistant that can show you a web page it has written: Claude (Artifacts), ChatGPT (Canvas) or Gemini (Canvas).
  • Download the workshop folder and keep the four tables at hand. You attach them to the chat in step 1.
  • Send one prompt at a time. Read what comes back before you send the next one.
  • When something looks wrong, say what you see in plain words: "the map is empty", "the total does not match". That is all the debugging you need.

The four tables

All of them come from the open data portal of the Ajuntament de València. They were prepared for this workshop so that each one opens in a spreadsheet and fits in a chat.

FileOne row isWhat it tells you
valenbisi_stations.csvA bike share station (273)Address, neighbourhood, district, position, bikes available, free docks and docks installed at the moment of capture.
neighbourhoods.csvA neighbourhood (88)Area, bike share stations and docks, bus stops and lines, kilometres of cycle route, and the 2021 vulnerability level.
city_history.csvA capture, every 10 minutesCitywide bikes available, empty stations, full stations and traffic through the day.
traffic_segments.csvA monitored street segment (392)Street name, latest sensor reading, average daily intensity and position.

Also in the folder: neighbourhood_outlines.geojson for the stretch goal, and dashboard_example.html, a finished example you can open in your browser.

The nine prompts

1

Meet the data before you build

8 minutes

Attach valenbisi_stations.csv and neighbourhoods.csv. A dashboard built on data you have not looked at will mislead you with confidence.

Prompt
I am an urban planner, not a programmer. I have attached two tables from the València open data portal: one row per public bike share station, and one row per neighbourhood.

Before building anything, tell me in plain language:
1. What each column means and its unit.
2. How many rows each table has, and how the two tables connect.
3. Anything odd in the data: missing values, stations where bikes plus free docks do not add up to the docks installed, neighbourhoods with no stations.
4. Three questions a city mobility officer could answer with these tables.

Do not write any code yet.

Look for: did it notice that some docks are out of use, and that the vulnerability level is missing for some neighbourhoods?

2

Agree the question and four numbers

8 minutes

A dashboard answers one question. Decide it now, and agree the headline numbers before any design exists.

Prompt
The question my dashboard must answer is: where is the bike share system running short right now?

A station can fail a user in two ways: no bikes to take, or no free dock to return one.

Propose four headline indicators for the top of the dashboard. For each one give me: a short name, how it is calculated in one sentence, and its value calculated from the attached file. Show the calculation so I can check it.

Do not build the dashboard yet.

Look for: numbers you can verify yourself. Open the table in a spreadsheet and count the stations with zero bikes.

3

First version: the numbers and the map

12 minutes

Now build. Ask for a small first version and say exactly what each mark on the map means.

Prompt
Build the first version of the dashboard as one self-contained web page, with the data from the stations table embedded in it, so it works without an internet connection.

Include only:
1. A title and one sentence stating the question the dashboard answers.
2. The four indicators we agreed, at the top.
3. A map of the stations, drawn directly from the longitude and latitude columns as points. Do not use map tiles or external map libraries.
   - Colour by state: no bikes, under 25% full, balanced, over 75% full, no free docks. Empty and full are both problems, so give them the two strongest colours.
   - Size by the number of docks installed.
   - Hovering a station shows its address, bikes available and free docks.
4. A legend, and a footer naming the source: Ajuntament de València open data portal.

Keep the design clean and readable. No other charts yet.

Look for: the points should draw the shape of the city, with the coast on the right. If the map looks stretched or mirrored, tell the assistant.

4

Where to act: the ranking

8 minutes

A map shows a pattern. A ranking tells an operator where to send the van first.

Prompt
Add a panel next to the map titled "Stations to rebalance now" with two short lists:
1. Stations with no bikes, largest stations first.
2. Stations with no free docks, largest stations first.

Show the address, the neighbourhood and the number of docks. Selecting a station in a list highlights it on the map.

Below the lists, add a bar chart of the ten neighbourhoods with the highest share of empty stations. Only include neighbourhoods with at least three stations, and say so in a note under the chart.

Look for: why the "at least three stations" rule matters. A neighbourhood with one station that happens to be empty would otherwise top the ranking at 100%.

5

One filter that moves everything

8 minutes

Interactivity is useful when the whole page answers the same question for a smaller place.

Prompt
Add a district filter at the top of the dashboard. When I choose a district, the four indicators, the map, the lists and the bar chart must all update to show only that district. Stations outside it stay on the map but faded.

Add a clear way to go back to the whole city.

Look for: choose a district and check that the indicators change. A filter that updates the map but not the numbers is the most common mistake.

6

Second layer: coverage and equity

12 minutes

The first layer asked "how is it working now". The second asks "who has access at all". This is where the neighbourhoods table comes in.

Prompt
Add a second view called "Coverage", reachable from a tab at the top, using the neighbourhoods table.

1. Calculate bike share docks per square kilometre and bus stops per square kilometre for each neighbourhood.
2. Show a scatter chart: docks per square kilometre on one axis, bus stops per square kilometre on the other, one dot per neighbourhood, coloured by vulnerability level. Neighbourhoods without a published vulnerability level are grey.
3. Show a sortable table of all neighbourhoods with these figures.
4. At the top of the view, write one sentence answering: which high vulnerability neighbourhoods have the least bike share coverage? Name them.

Note: in this dataset a lower vulnerability index means more vulnerable, so use the vulnerability level column (Low, Medium, High) for labels and colours.

Look for: the sentence at the top. Does it name real neighbourhoods from the table, and do the figures in the table support it?

7

Time: the day so far

8 minutes

Attach city_history.csv. The city only publishes the present moment. This table exists because a small script has been saving a copy every 10 minutes.

Prompt
I have attached a new table with one row per capture, taken every 10 minutes today.

Add a section called "Through the day" to the first view with two line charts:
1. Bikes available in the whole city.
2. Stations with no bikes and stations with no free docks, as two lines.

Label the time axis in hours and minutes. Under the charts, write one sentence describing what changed between the first and the last capture.

Look for: the vertical axis. If it does not start at zero, a small change can look dramatic. Ask the assistant to explain its choice.

8

Check the numbers

5 minutes

The step most people skip, and the one that makes the work yours.

Prompt
Audit your own dashboard. Recalculate each of the four headline indicators directly from the stations table I attached, step by step, and compare each result with the number the dashboard displays.

List every difference you find, explain its cause, and fix it. Then tell me which figures on the dashboard I should double check by hand, and how.

Look for: an honest answer. If it reports no differences, test one figure yourself in a spreadsheet anyway.

9

From dashboard to decision

6 minutes

A dashboard is finished when someone can act on it.

Prompt
Using only what this dashboard shows, write a short briefing for the city mobility officer:

1. Three findings, each one sentence long and each supported by a specific number from the dashboard.
2. One action for this week and one for the next investment plan.
3. Two limits of this data that the officer must keep in mind before deciding.

Add the briefing as a section at the end of the dashboard titled "What this means".

Look for: findings that follow from the numbers. Remove any claim the data cannot support, such as reasons why a station is empty.

If you have time left

Traffic layer
I have attached traffic_segments.csv, with the latest sensor reading for each monitored street segment. Add a third view called "Traffic" with the fifteen busiest segments as a bar chart, and plot the segments as points on the same map, sized by the reading. The portal does not state the unit of the reading, so label it "latest sensor reading".
Neighbourhood outlines
I have attached neighbourhood_outlines.geojson. Draw the neighbourhood outlines behind the stations on the map, and in the Coverage view shade each neighbourhood by docks per square kilometre. Join it to the neighbourhoods table by the "code" column.
Keep it up to date
The source data changes every 10 minutes. Explain, for someone who is not a programmer, how this dashboard could refresh itself every day without anyone touching it. Describe the simplest option step by step, what it costs, and what could break.

Five habits worth keeping

  • Question first, charts second. Write the question at the top of the page and remove anything that does not help answer it.
  • One change per prompt. Small steps are easy to check and easy to undo.
  • Say what every mark means: colour, size and position each carry one thing.
  • Ask it to explain before you trust it. "How did you calculate this?" is always a fair question.
  • Name your sources and the time of capture. A number without a date is a rumour.

Data: Ajuntament de València open data portal (opendata.vlci.valencia.es), datasets ValenBisi Disponibilidad, Intensidad tráfico tramos, Barrios, EMT, Itinerarios ciclistas and Vulnerabilidad por barrios 2021. Tables prepared by CITIES FORUM for teaching.
